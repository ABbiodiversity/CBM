Project Introduction

Members of the Otipemisiwak Métis Government Lac La Biche District 19 (OMGD19) are interested in understanding the changes in wildlife condition and behaviour that they are noticing on the land. The health and abundance of wildlife populations is relied upon by members of OMGD19 to support a traditional way of life. To ensure the sustainability of this way of life it is important to understand both how many animals, such as moose, are available for harvest every year and what the drivers of change are in the abundance of wildlife over time. These concerns led to the initiation of this wildlife monitoring program in 2023.

In 2023, OMGD19 created a Community Knowledge Committee comprising both elders and youths to lead this project. This Community Knowledge Committee shares knowledge and disseminates information about the program across age ranges and the region. The Committee engages with ABMI staff about program updates, monitoring methods, study design, site locations, analysis, and integration of Indigenous knowledge indicators.



The Committee deployed 18 cameras between late September 2023 and October 2024. These cameras were primarily deployed along the Old Conklin Road near Lac La Biche (see Figure below) in clusters of 4 cameras. Cameras were deployed based on the knowledge of the land, areas of interest by the committee, and areas of oil sands disturbance.

The goal of the program is to evaluate the specific oil sands disturbance type of pipelines and roads compared to reference (low disturbance) areas. To achieve this, 2 clusters of 4 cameras were deployed along pipeline right of ways and roads at distances up to 100 metres. Additionally, 2 clusters of cameras were deployed in reference areas picked by the community.

Camera Refresh and Image Processing

The wildlife cameras were refreshed (given new SD cards and batteries) in the field by the Community Knowledge Committee in 2024 and 2025. The CKC members uploaded and tagged all of the camera images on the online platform WildTrax1. WildTrax is an online platform developed by the Alberta Biodiversity Monitoring Institute for users of environmental sensors such as remote cameras to provide support with processing, standardizing, and sharing data. Initial results from this first year of data collected at the 18 cameras are presented here in this report.

Independent Detections Over Time

With camera data, one useful metric to track over time is the number of “independent” detections captured for each species. In this case, an independent detection is defined as an image or collection of images captured for a given species that can be thought of as a single detection event.

In the figure below we present the number of independent detections for each of the 8 focal species detected in the OMGD19 project. We use a 30 minute time interval to separate detection events; that is, images captured within 30 minutes of each other count toward a single detection and images with a gap of greater than 30 minutes between them count as multiple independent detections. Images that are captured of the same species over less than 30 minutes are assumed to be of the same individual, or group of individuals.

Temporal Activity Patterns

Given that camera traps operate 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, and can record animal motion down to second-level precision, they represent a powerful tool to explore and contrast the activity patterns of the species they detect. Such analyses can give insight into competition, predation, and coexistence.

In this section we present the temporal (diel) activity patterns for each of the 8 focal species from the OMGD19 cameras. The x-axis of each of the following plots represents the 24-hr daily cycle, and the line represents the relative activity level detected by the cameras. This data is pooled across all 18 cameras in the project.

  • White-tailed Deer
  • Black Bear
  • Moose
  • Coyote
  • Snowshoe Hare
  • Canada Lynx
  • Woodland Caribou
  • Gray Wolf
  • White-tailed deer are primarily active during twilight hours, specifically at dawn and dusk. This pattern is known as crepuscular. The majority of the activity, however, was captured during the dawn hours (6:00 to 9:00).
  • Black Bear were similarily crepuscular, although the peak was much more pronounced during the dusk hours (between 18:00 and 24:00).
  • Based on the cameras in the OMGD19 project, Moose were primarily active during the night time (00:00 to 6:00).
  • Coyote and Snowshoe Hare were similar to Moose, but had slightly elevated (and increasing) activity levels throughout the evening hours.
  • Canada Lynx activity peaked during the midday (12:00). Caribou were similar, with a slightly earlier, and more pronounced, peak (10:00).
  • Finally, Gray Wolf showed a slightly crepuscular tendency, however the dusk activity peak was more extreme.

Species Density

In addition to the number of images and counts of independent detections, we can estimate the density of each species at each camera. Density is the number of animals per unit area, which translates to abundance when scaled up to a larger region of interest.

To estimate the density of each wildlife species at each camera, we used the Time in Front of Camera (TIFC) approach (Becker et al. (2022)). This method has previously been used to estimate densities of unmarked populations of both white-tailed deer and moose in the boreal region of Alberta. Similar to quadrat sampling, the TIFC approach involves (for each camera) multiplying the number of animals observed by the total time they spend in front of the camera, which is then divided by the product of the area sampled by the camera and the total time the camera was operating. This calculation yields an estimate of animals per unit area (i.e., ‘density’).

Evaluating Oil Sands Disturbances

In this section we present the results from OMGD19 camera deployments evaluating the impact of specific oil sands stressors on wildlife within OMGD19: pipelines and roads. The effect of these stressors on species’ density is evaluated by comparing values to a reference (low disturbance) area.

The following plots show the density of each species at each cluster of cameras. Each point represents the average density value among the cameras of the cluster. For instance, the 4 cameras that belong to the ‘MNA 19-6’ cluster (which is a reference cluster) had white-tailed deer density values of 4.75, 4.79, 5, and 3.36 animals per km2, for an average density of 4.47.   • White-tailed deer have the highest densities across both stressor treatments and the reference areas among all the species. Density was highest in the reference clusters, and lower in both the linear features cluster and the two roads clusters.
  • The opposite pattern was found for Black Bear, where lower densities were found in the reference clusters and higher densities in the stressor treatments, particularly the two road clusters.
  • Moose densities were relatively low across all camera clusters, especially in areas of linear features (pipelines).
  • Coyotes were attracted to to road features, but not linear features. The same pattern was observed for Snowshoe Hare and Canada Lynx.
  • Caribou and Wolf densities (and overall detections) were very low across all clusters, making it difficult to assess patterns and response to stressors. However, wolf density does seem to be higher in reference areas.

Species Predictions

The ABMI compiles all remote camera data collected in Alberta’s boreal region (including the Oil Sands Region) and uses it to develop habitat models for each species. These models incorporate species’ responses to land cover (e.g., vegetation and forest types), human disturbance, and climate variables.
