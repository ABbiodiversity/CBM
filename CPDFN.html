Project Introduction

The Chipewyan Prairie Dene First Nation (CPDFN) OSM camera program started in 2022 as a focus study looking at the use of industrial Right-of-Ways (RoWs) by non-local and non-Indigenous land users. The camera program then shifted to be part of the regional ICMB program designed to answer questions and concerns expressed by the community such as understanding how wildlife abundances change in proximity to human disturbance (including oil sands industry disturbance). This webpage will focus on the results from both the Human Land Use and Wildlife Abundance study designs.

Human Land Use Study

CPDFN members have been experiencing the impacts of oil sands development since around 2000. Land users have seen firsthand how these developments are changing the way they access and use the land. Some of the harvesting sites have been directly disturbed and destroyed. Others are still there, but getting to them has become harder—either because access is blocked, uncomfortable, or just not appropriate anymore.

At the same time, they’re seeing more and more outsiders—like oil sands workers staying in camps, or non-Indigenous hunters and recreational users—coming onto their land. They’re using roads and trails built for oil sands development to get into areas used traditionally for harvesting and cultural activities. This has led to increased competition for land and resources, and sometimes even dangerous or racist interactions, vandalism, littering, and poaching. CPDFN members are also dealing with loud noise, bad smells, heavy traffic, bright lights, and other things that ruin the peaceful experience of being out on the land.

CPDFN has been raising these concerns with oil sands companies for years, but often, they don’t take them seriously. So CPDFN decided to collect their own evidence. From 2022 to 2024, led by Stuart Janvier, Louie Janvier, Caiden Morice and Ave Dersch with support from ABMI, CPDFN ran a camera monitoring project to track who’s coming into their territory and what kinds of impacts they’re seeing. This section of the report shares the results of that project and helps show what they already knew—yes, oil sands development has created pathways for outsiders to come onto their land, and yes, it’s causing real problems for their community.

Wildlife Abundance Study

In 2024 the cameras used in the Human Land Use study were retrieved and then deployed along the 20th Baseline to monitor wildlife. The 20th Baseline runs west to east with the Black Gold Sag-D plant to the West (high oil sands disturbance) and the Dillon River Wildland with considerably less disturbance to the east (some old cutlines and occasional conventional well pads). The objective of this study is to document and compare the wildlife abundances calculated from the images collected from each camera deployed along the baseline to see if wildlife use the landscape differently when closer or further away from the area of high disturbance.

Unfortunately during the spring of 2023 a large wildfire burned through the area and a number of cameras were lost. Cameras were re-deployed afterwards and the program continued.

Camera Refresh and Image Processing

The human land use cameras were refreshed (given new SD cards and batteries) in the field by CPDFN in December 2022. The images from the SD cards were uploaded and tagged on the online platform WildTrax. WildTrax is an online platform developed by the Alberta Biodiversity Monitoring Institute for users of environmental sensors such as remote cameras to provide support with processing, organizing, and sharing data.

CPDFN have 4 projects available on WildTrax with the images stored and tagged:

  • CPDFN Wildlife Cameras: Contains images from the 18 cameras initially deployed for the Human Land Use study in January 2022 and refreshed in November later that year.
  • CPDFN Wildlife Cameras 2023: Contains images from the same 18 cameras over the next year (November 2022 to November 2023). Also includes a new location that monitors a salt lick identified by the community that is highly trafficked by wildlife.
  • CPFN 20th Baseline Cameras: This project contains images from 13 cameras deployed for the Wildlife Abundance project along 20th baseline.
  • 20th Baseline Cameras 2025: This project contains images from 20 cameras deployed further along 20th baseline for the Wildlife Abundance portion of the project.

Note that viewers will need to have a WildTrax account with the appropriate permissions from the community and be logged in to see the these images.

Results

Human Land Use

We first present the results from the Human Land Use Study.

Number of Images

Figure 2 below displays the total number of images collected with the cameras used for the Human Land Use component of the CPDFN monitoring. The most commonly observed species was White-tailed Deer with 34,895 images collected with these cameras. Moose were the second most common species seen with these cameras, with 14,804 images collected (with many of these images collected at the Salt Lick site).

Vehicles, Humans, and All Terrain vehicles were also commonly tagged in the images.

Resident vs Non-Resident Detections

The first important component of this study was to identify the number of CPDFN residents and non-residents who were accessing the land. The images from the cameras were tagged by Louie Janvier and Caiden Morice to identify residents from non-residents. Those who were actively hunting (i.e., armed with a rifle, firearm, compound bow) were further identified in the tagging process. These images were then grouped together into ‘independent detections’ based on a 30 minute separation of time between images threshold.

Figure 3 below show the total number of independent detections at each of the 16 cameras in this part of the study. Independent detections were defined as series of images of a single event (e.g., a passing group of non-residents). A threshold of 10 minutes between images was used to delineate separate detection events. The vast majority of human detections across all cameras were non-residents (728; 94%) versus a small number of residents (46; 6%). CPDFN-13 was the most frequently visited site with nearly 200 total detections, followed by CPDFN-4 (106) and CPDFN-12 (87). CPDFN-9 was the only site that where 0 non-resident detections were recorded.



Next we can plot these detections of both CPDFN residents and non-residents over time. Pooling the detections across all 16 cameras, Figure 4 below shows the number of detections across the time period in which these cameras were operational. The figure is split into two panels, with the top showing CPDFN residents and the bottom showing non-residents. In this figure, the purple color shows detections that were tagged as being resident hunters, and the dark green color shows non-resident hunters.

In both 2022 and 2023, the highest number of detections (both resident and non-resident) occurred during November (corresponding with the primary non-Indigenous hunting season). Detections were much lower throughout the rest of the winter and into the summer. In both years, a significant proportion of non-resident detections could be identified as hunters.

Another part of the tagging process involved assessing the mode of transportation for each of the detections. The table below lists each combination of residency, whether or not they were seen to be hunting, and the transportation mode they used.

Quads and side-by-sides were commonly used by non-residents (both hunters and non-hunters alike) to access the land. Argos and skidoos were less commonly used, likely reflecting the seasonal conditions that most users encountered.


Wildlife Abundance

The following sections summarise the results from the Wildlife Abundance study conducted by CPDFN.

Number of Images

Figure 5 below displays the total number of images collected with the cameras used for the Wildlife Abundance component of the CPDFN monitoring. The most commonly observed species was White-tailed Deer with 2,584 images collected. Woodland Caribou were the second most common species seen with these cameras, with 2,048 images collected, followed by Moose with 1,714. Black Bears, Gray Wolves, Snowshoe Hares, and Canada Lynx were also observed with the wildlife cameras.

Independent Detections Over Time

The number of images of each species tells us part of the picture but just because there may be more images of one species doesn’t necessarily mean there are more of them in the area, they may have just spent more time in front of the camera. With camera data, it’s important to track the number of “independent” detections for each species. Like what was done for the Human Land Use study results, you can think of an independent detection as an image or collection of images for a visit from an animal or group of animals.

In the figures below we present the number of independent detections for each of the 7 focal species detected in the CPDFN project. We use a 30 minute time interval to separate detection events; that is, images captured within 30 minutes of each other count toward a single detection and images with a gap of greater than 30 minutes between them count as multiple independent detections. Images that are captured of the same species over less than 30 minutes are assumed to be of the same individual, or group of individuals.

The y-axis (left) shows the number of detections captured, from all of the cameras from the Wildlife Abundance study. One thing to keep in mind when evaluating the number of detections over time is that the effort (in terms of number of cameras operating) was not constant. See the Deployment Retrieval Dates section for information on the number of cameras in operation at any given time.

Temporal Activity Patterns

Since camera traps take photos continuously 24 hours a day they can tell us when different species are active. These analyses can give insight into competition, predation, and coexistence.

In this section we present the temporal (diel) activity patterns for each of the 7 focal species from the CPDFN cameras. The x-axis (bottom) of each of the following plots represents the 24-hr daily cycle, and the line represents the relative activity level detected by the cameras. This data is pooled across all 29 cameras in this study.

  • White-tailed Deer
  • Black Bear
  • Moose
  • Snowshoe Hare
  • Canada Lynx
  • Woodland Caribou
  • Gray Wolf


  • White-tailed deer displayed several activity peaks throughout the day, the most prominent of which were during twilight hours, specifically at dawn (6:00 to 9:00) and dusk (19:00 to 21:00). This pattern is known as crepuscular.
  • Black Bear showed the opposite pattern, with more of their activity being recorded during the day. Their activity levels increased throughout the morning (6:00 to 12:00), peaking at noon, and then declining throughout the afternoon and into the evening.
  • Similar to White-tailed Deer, Moose demonstrated a crepuscular activity pattern, with peaks in the dawn and dusk. A notable peak occurred before 24:00 as well before falling throughout the nighttime period.
  • Snowshoe Hare activity increased throughout the evening beginning at 18:00, carrying throughout the night until dawn. They were not active during the day. Canada Lynx activity levels were fairly consistent throughout the 24-hour period, with a peak occurring in the mid-afternoon (14:00).
  • Gray Wolves had one significant activity peak: the dusk period from about 20:00 to 24:00.
  • Woodland Caribou activity spiked in the late morning period, with a significant peak prior to 12:00. Activity levels were very low at other periods of the day, with very few images/detections recorded at any other time. This pattern may be due to predator avoidance, especially since gray wolves were active at the opposite times.

Species Overlap

Camera trap data are being increasingly used to model multiple species communities, and we can use this data to explore the co-occurrence patterns of the species in the community.

Blue boxes in Figure 6 below show species that are usually seen by the same cameras at the same location. For example, Moose and White-tailed Deer often share the same space because they browse on similar vegetation and use the same habitats.

Red (or white) boxes show species that tend to stay apart and weren’t usually seen at the same camera. For example, Woodland Caribou have distinct habitat preferences - e.g., wet, lowland sites – that results in spatial separation between them and other ungulates (moose and deer) as well as predators (bears and wolves). Similarly, moose were found to avoid sites that wolves were seen at.

Species Density

We can use the number of images and counts of independent detections to estimate the density of each species at each camera. Density is the number of animals per unit area, which we express below as the number of animals per square kilometer (km2).

To estimate the density of each wildlife species at each camera, we used the Time in Front of Camera (TIFC) approach (Becker et al. (2022)). This method has previously been used to estimate densities of unmarked populations of both White-tailed Deer and Moose in the boreal region of Alberta (Laurent et al. (2021), Dickie et al. (2024)). Similar to quadrat sampling, this approach involves (for each camera) multiplying the number of animals observed by the total time they spend in front of the camera, which is then divided by the product of the area sampled by the camera and the total time the camera was operating. This calculation yields an estimate of animals per unit area (i.e., ‘density’).

The following map displays the spatial distribution in densities of the 7 focal species between the 29 cameras of the CPDFN camera wildlife abundance project. Note that cameras with no detections of a particular species (i.e., a density of ‘0’) are represented with a black circle. Smaller circles represent small densities of a species at that camera. Bigger circles indicate that the density is higher at that camera.

Comparison to Regional Oil Sands Monitoring

We can take the calculated species density values from the CPDFN cameras and compare the values to those collected from cameras deployed elsewhere in the Oil Sands Region. The regional monitoring program uses a dose-response study design, where data is collected at various oil sands footprint stressors (e.g., active well sites, mines, roads, and seismic lines) and then this data is compared to areas of low disturbance (Bayne et al. (2021)). For the CPDFN wildlife abundance study, cameras were deployed at two oil sands stressor treatments (linear features and inactive wellpads) as well as low disturbance (reference) areas.

The following plots (Figures 7a-7g) show the average density of each species found at each stressor compared to the reference areas. The plots also show the range of values obtained through the broader regional monitoring program; the average density value for each stressor treatment is represented by a horizontal dark grey line on the plots. When comparing CPDFN values to these averages, it is important to note that these are pooled averages from sites surveyed across the entire Oil Sands Region, and represent a variety of geographic characteristics.

Tip

Click on each Data Source (e.g., ‘OSM Regional Monitoring’) within each of the plot legends to hide or display the data associated with each source.

  • White-tailed Deer
  • Black Bear
  • Moose
  • Snowshoe Hare
  • Canada Lynx
  • Woodland Caribou
  • Gray Wolf

  • White-tailed deer density was lowest in the linear features treatment, and comparable between the inactive wellpads treatment and the reference areas. Inactive wellpads can provide early seral vegetation for white-tailed deer to browse on, which in effect creates a forage subsidy. Across both treatments and the reference areas, however, white-tailed deer densities were lower at CPDFN cameras compared to the regional averages. This may be due to the underlying habitat present in this localized area (e.g., a higher proportion of lowland area compared to upland deciduous/mixedwood, which deer prefer).

  • A similar pattern was found for Black Bear, with inactive wellpads and reference areas have similar density values, but areas with a high concentration of linear features having lower numbers of bears. Like white-tailed deer, black bear densities were lower in the CPDFN area than regional averages.

  • Regionally, Moose have either a neutral or slight positive response to footprint such as linear features and inactive wellpads; like white-tailed deer, these features provide a forage and/or movement subsidy. However, this finding was not replicated with the CPDFN data, as moose density was lower than reference in both treatments (albeit with a small sample size). Like white-tailed deer and black bear, moose densities were significantly lower than regional averages.

  • Snowshoe Hare were not found in the inactive wellpad treatment, but had similar densities between the linear features treatment and the reference areas. At the latter two, hare densities were similar to regional averages. Canada Lynx densities at CPDFN sites across both treatments and the reference areas were higher than regional averages.

  • High densities of Woodland Caribou were found at the CPDFN reference sites – higher than any other sites sampled regionally. These sites coincide with East Side Athabasca (ESAR) Christina subherd range. Caribou densities were lower in the two oil sands footprint treatments when compared to reference.

  • Finally, Gray Wolf densities were higher in reference areas than the two treatments. CPDFN cameras recorded higher densities in these areas than the regional average.

Where We Expect to Find Species

The ABMI compiles remote camera data collected in Alberta’s boreal region (including the Oil Sands Region) and uses it to develop habitat models for many species. These models incorporate species’ responses to land cover (e.g., vegetation and forest types), human disturbance, and climate variables (seasonal weather patterns).

The maps below show where the models predict each species is most likely to be found in the CPDFN area of interest. The area is divided into 1 km2 pixels, and for each pixel, the model estimates the probability of each species being there based on the habitat and disturbance present in that pixel.

Use the buttons on the top right of the map to toggle through each species’ predictions. You can also turn on Satellite Imagery and use the NONE (Blank) button to view the imagery underneath the predictions, as well as use the Camera Locations button to see where CPDFN camera locations are.


  • White-tailed Deer are far more likely to be found in the areas where the Human Land Use cameras were deployed due to the configuration of habitat and increased human disturbance. These predictions were validated by the camera data collected there and in both CPDFN studies. Moose show a similar pattern to white-tailed deer.

  • Black Bear have a high prediction of occurrence throughout the CPDFN study area.

  • Snowshoe Hare, and to a lesser extent Canada Lynx, thrive in the lowland habitats that make up a significant portion of the east side of the CPDFN area of interest.

  • Woodland Caribou prediction of occurrence in patchy due to their range limitations. The ESAR Christina and Bohn subherds are prominent in the CPDFN territory. Wolves have low predictions of occurrence, but that is because they exist in low densities across the landscape.

Recommendations and Next Steps

CPDFN plans to continue being part of the regional ICBM camera program. We look forward to building out more indicators with hunters related to wildlife health.
